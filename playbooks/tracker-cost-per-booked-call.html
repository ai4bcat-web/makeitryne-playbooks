The cost-per-booked-call tracker

Your $20 leads might be $400 booked calls. Three numbers a week tell you what your ads really cost, and which half of the funnel to fix.

The math nobody runs

The ads manager shows you cost per lead. That's the number it wants you to judge. Here's the one that pays the bills: if one lead in twenty books a call, a $20 lead is a $400 booked call. Same ads, same budget, book one in ten instead, and that call drops to $200.

Cheap leads that never book are the most expensive leads you'll ever buy.

The three numbers

1. Cost per lead

Ad spend for the week divided by leads (comments, DMs, form fills, calls) that week. Straight from the ads manager.

2. Book rate

Leads that actually got on a call, divided by leads. Count booked and held, not booked and ghosted.

3. Cost per booked call

Ad spend divided by booked calls. Or, the quick way: cost per lead divided by book rate. This is the number you judge the ad by.

cost per booked call = cost per lead ÷ book rate $20 ÷ 0.05 (1 in 20) = $400 $20 ÷ 0.10 (1 in 10) = $200

Add one more column if you can: "closed". Then you'll know cost per closed job, which is the only number that matters more than this one.

How to read it

1
Cost per lead up, book rate steady: the ad is the problem. Swap the hook or the audience.
2
Cost per lead fine, book rate under 1 in 10: the problem is after the click. Reply speed, the two questions, the calendar link, the follow-ups. Most operators swap the ad here. Don't.
3
Book rate up, cost per booked call still high: you're booking the wrong people. Tighten the two qualifying questions in the DM.
4
Fill it in every Monday. Four weeks of rows beats any gut feeling about which ad "feels" better.
